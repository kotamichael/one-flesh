# Week 7 — "Loving Leadership" (~p. 101 onward)

The husband-facing parallel to ch. 6, and the other richest cross-reference week in this plan.

## What's actually in the chapter (CONFIRMED, direct quotes)

> "To be like Jesus Christ in relationship to your wife is an enormous order to fill. You are to
> be the head of your home, including your wife, just as Christ is the head of the church..." —
> p. 89 (note: this project's own prior research spliced this with the p. 101 quote below as one
> continuous passage — they're two separate quotes on two separate pages; cite them separately)

> "If love has grown cold in your family, husband, you must do something about it... You are the
> head of your home. If there is little or no love in that home, it is your fault. God holds you
> responsible to introduce love." — p. 101

Adams's headship is reduced to direct, actionable counseling directives rather than abstract
theological proposition — the recurring move across everything recovered from this book.

## Memory verses this week

**Eph. 5:25, 28-29 (KJV)** — the anchor text for the chapter's whole title and content:

> "Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it... So
> ought men to love their wives as their own bodies. He that loveth his wife loveth himself. For no
> man ever yet hated his own flesh; but nourisheth and cherisheth it, even as the Lord the church."

## Who Adams is arguing against here

Two candidates worth naming: a husband who treats headship as a title without active
responsibility (against which "you are the head... if there is little or no love in that home, it
is your fault" is a direct corrective); and, less certainly, an abdicating/passive model of male
leadership Adams may have encountered often enough in counseling to make this chapter's insistence
on active fault-taking necessary. Not confirmed as a named target in the text.

## Cross-references — your physical books first

**Calvin's Galatians/Ephesians commentary**, on 5:22-33: "Christ has appointed the same relation to
exist between a husband and a wife, as between himself and his church," with an explicit
reciprocal duty — "Let husbands imitate Christ... he scrupled not to die for his church." The
classic Reformed statement of exactly what Adams's "enormous order to fill" is gesturing at.

**Zanchi's *Spiritual Marriage* — the whole book, and its likely closest match to this chapter.**
Zanchi moves from Christ's headship over the church directly into applying "Head and everlasting
Savior" language to the husband's relation to his wife, on the express ground that Paul does the
same in Eph. 5:23. If Adams's chapter title is "Loving Leadership," Zanchi's entire treatise is the
deepest available primary-source argument for why *loving* is not incidental to headship but
definitional to it, on Christ's own pattern.

**Palmer's *The Family*, Ch. II ("Supremacy of the Husband") and Ch. IV ("The Symbolical Mystery of
Marriage").** Palmer opens ch. II by noting that "in all the apostolic injunctions, the great duty
enforced upon [the husband] is Love" (citing Col. 3:19; Eph. 5:25,28,33) — then asks, pointedly,
"But is not love as much the duty of the wife?" and works out why love is specifically emphasized
to the husband. A good direct comparison to whatever reasoning Adams gives for why the "fault"
language in this chapter falls on the husband specifically.

**Perkins' *Christian Oeconomie*.** Perkins restrains headship explicitly: "though the husband be
the wife's head, yet it seemeth he hath no power nor liberty granted him" to be cruel — worth
having open as a check on how Adams's "you are responsible... it is your fault" language avoids
tipping into either abdication (opposite failure) or harshness (same failure Perkins warns
against).

## Where the sources disagree — how is "weaker vessel" (1 Pet. 3:7) actually used?

Confirmed only for **Calvin** in Strand 1 (protective honor, not contempt — "the male sex is but
the half of the human race... they acknowledge themselves to be debtors to the weaker sex").
**Richard Steele** has the most vivid version: "wives are like crystal glasses, easily broken if
not tenderly handled." Whether **Adams** uses "weaker vessel" language at all, and how, is
currently unconfirmed in this project's research on him — worth listening for specifically this
week, since 3:7 is addressed to husbands and this is the husband-facing chapter.

## Where the sources disagree — Aristotelian vs. purely biblical categories for describing rule

**Vermigli** is unique in this whole bibliography for explicitly importing Aristotelian political
categories — marital rule as "the Dominion of Aristocratia" (rule by "the chief, which excelleth
in virtue and worthiness"), while conceding "this [does] not always come to pass" in practice. No
other figure in this project's research reaches for a philosophical category this explicitly
rather than working straight from the text — worth noting as a real methodological outlier, not a
representative move, if it comes up.

## This week's practice

**Directly from Adams's own material**: "if there is little or no love in that home, it is your
fault... God holds you responsible to introduce love" is already homework in Adams's own words.
This week, name one specific, concrete action — not a general resolution — to actively introduce
or rekindle love this week, rather than waiting for circumstances or your wife to initiate it.

**Heart-related, from Palmer and Calvin**: Palmer's insistence that the husband's love is "the
challenging love... the original," modeled on Christ's own initiating, self-giving love — sit with
this before doing the homework above. The point isn't performing a duty efficiently; it's
examining whether your own heart actually initiates love the way Christ's does, or waits to be
moved first.

## Things to look for / discussion questions

- Per this project's symmetric-treatment discipline: does this chapter name the husband's
  characteristic besetting sins (harshness, passivity/abdication, self-seeking) with the same
  directness ch. 6 should give the wife's, or does it stay mostly at the positive-duty level?
  Worth tracking explicitly, since this is exactly the axis this project's house rules were
  revised to watch for.
- Compare Adams's "it is your fault" language directly to Gouge's "most marital misery comes from
  an husband's abuse of his authority" — is Adams naming the same failure mode (abuse/harshness),
  the opposite one (passivity/abdication), or holding both together the way Gouge does?
- A natural closing discussion question for weeks 6-7 together: having read both chapters, does
  Adams's account of headship and submission read as symmetric — full role, responsibility,
  warning, and pastoral counsel for both spouses — or does one side get fuller treatment? This is
  the single most useful comparative question this whole plan can ask.
