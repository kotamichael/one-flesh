# Week 4 — "Bible Basics About the Family" (pp. 43-57)

This is the doctrinal core of the book and the richest cross-reference week in this whole plan —
it's where Adams lays out the foundational theology the rest of the book's practical counsel
stands on.

## What's actually in the chapter (CONFIRMED via the project owner's direct read, 2026-09-06)

The earlier gracequotes.org-reconstructed guess is superseded — this section now follows Adams's
own structure directly, per the project's house rule to track a source's own categories rather
than impose an external grid. Adams organizes the chapter as **three "basics," plus a set of
"overlooked aspects"**:

**Basic 1 — God ordained marriage** (p. 44, "The family is foundational; it was first because it
is basic"). Marriage is not optional, not time-bound, and not outmoded by modern innovations
(Adams names abortion and birth control specifically). Marriage is **covenantal** — "unilaterally
imposed," carrying its own blessings and sanctions, not a negotiated contract between equals.
Cites Prov. 2:17 and Mal. 2:14.

**Basic 2 — marriage is good** (p. 45). Grounded pre-Fall, not a "lesser of two evils" concession
to sin: sex itself is good, and marriage is "given to bless and make happy and joyful." Cites Heb.
13:4 (marriage "undefiled" — implying it's capable of being defiled, but is not defiling in
itself), Eph. 5:22-33, Rev. 19:7-9 and 21:2 (marriage supper/bride imagery), and reads 1 Cor. 7:26
as situational counsel bound to "the present distress," not a timeless devaluation of marriage.
Gen. 2:18 grounds marriage, not celibacy, as the normal state — celibacy the exception.

**Basic 3 — why was woman created?** (following Gen. 2:18's "help meet"). Adams reads *ezer
kenegdo* as suitability, complementarity, correspondence to the man — and makes an explicit,
polemical move here: **modernity has lost this vision, and the woman's-liberation movement, far
from liberating women, actually enslaves them by subverting it** — true liberation, on Adams's
account, comes from a woman *correctly* inhabiting this role, not rejecting it. Man and wife
together become one flesh, a single complete unit. How the wife helps the husband, per this
chapter: companionship, and biologically (sex) — **neither party has a right over their own
body**, self-orientation in sex is "perversion" in any form it takes, "it is more blessed to give
than to receive," and this applies to both partners actively seeking the *other's* satisfaction,
not just their own. (This is Adams's own version of 1 Cor. 7:3-5's mutual-authority-over-the-body
logic, though he does not cite chapter and verse for it directly in the notes taken.)

**Reproduction.** Ps. 127 ("quiver full of arrows"); Gen. 1:28, read directly as blessing rather
than bare command: "I give you my blessing to bear children; fill the earth and subdue it" (p. 51).

**Overlooked aspects — the marital bond's priority over the parent-child bond.** "Man will leave
father and mother" — Gen. 2:24's own language — grounds Adams's strongest claim in the chapter:
**"Marriage is the most basic unit in society... Perhaps nothing that will be said in this book is
more important than this"** (p. 51). The husband becomes head of a "new decision-making unit." Adams
argues society inverts this, over-elevating the parent-child bond above the marital one, and
diagnoses the cost directly from his own casework: **"Parents who have built their lives around
their children frequently end up in our counseling center around the time when the last child is
leaving home"** (p. 52). Children need to see parents who know how to love each other well, not
just parent well. His pointed question to couples: "what kind of life would you have right now
were your children suddenly taken from you?... What have you really built between you?" (p. 55).
The responsibility to protect the marriage against this drift **falls specifically on the husband,
as head of the home** — "Christ has made it *your* job" (p. 56). The chapter's homework is a
family time/interest distribution chart, quantifying (with a caveat that quality matters as much
as quantity) how time and interest are actually divided between spouse and children (p. 57).

**On the standing "equal doctrinal depth" question**: now answerable, not just trackable. The
chapter gives the wife's side real content (the helper/complementarity doctrine, the polemic
against women's-lib, her active role in mutual sexual self-giving) — but it is thinner and more
instrumentally framed (what she does *for* the marriage/the husband) than the husband's side,
which gets the chapter's single strongest, most emphatic claim ("perhaps nothing... more
important") and the explicit headship-responsibility assignment for guarding the marriage against
child-centered drift. Worth naming honestly rather than smoothing over, per the project's own
symmetric-treatment discipline — this chapter is not a violation of that rule (ch. 6 is still
coming, addressed to wives specifically), but on its own it leans husband-heavier in its climactic
material.

## Who Adams is arguing against here (CONFIRMED, sharpened 2026-09-06)

Two real, nameable targets now confirmed directly in the chapter's own text — not the vague
"broadly therapeutic/secular" placeholder the earlier guess offered:

**The women's-liberation movement, explicitly.** Writing in 1972, at the height of second-wave
feminism (Friedan's *The Feminine Mystique*, 1963; NOW founded 1966; the ERA fight underway),
Adams directly inverts the movement's own claim to speak for women's freedom: correctly
inhabiting the God-given helper role is what actually *liberates* a woman, and the women's-lib
project, by subverting that role, *enslaves* her instead. This is a real, dateable, textually
confirmed polemical target — the sharpest one this project has found anywhere in Adams's corpus so
far — and it belongs specifically to Basic 3 (why woman was created), not the whole chapter.

**A softer, second target: child-centered family culture.** Not named as a movement or named
opponent the way women's-lib is, but Adams diagnoses a real cultural pattern from his own
counseling caseload — couples who structure their whole lives around their children, at the
marriage's expense, and end up in crisis when the children leave. This reads as an observation
from pastoral experience rather than a polemic against a named ideology, but it functions the same
way structurally: a wrong ordering of family life Adams is writing to correct.

Also still live, more diffusely: the chapter's covenantal framing of marriage ("unilaterally
imposed," with blessings and sanctions, not a negotiated contract) is a quiet rebuttal of any view
of marriage as a mutable social contract — consistent with the whole book's occasion (see ch. 1-2
notes) but not singled out as sharply as the two targets above.

## Where the sources disagree — the big one: does headship originate at creation, or only at the Fall?

This is the sharpest fault line in this project's entire bibliography, and this "basics" chapter is
the natural place to bring it into the room, since it's exactly the kind of foundational question a
"basics" chapter has to take some position on (even if only implicitly).

- **Creation-only, sharply**: **Musculus** draws the starkest line of anyone in this bibliography —
  Gen. 2:24 (the pre-Fall "law of wedlock") has *no rule-language at all*; a distinct, explicitly
  post-Fall "law" (Gen. 3:16) is what first introduces "the woman is commanded to be subject."
- **Creation-grounded, fall-confirms**: **Calvin**'s "gentle subjection, then servitude" — headship
  *predates* the Fall but the Fall *intensifies* it. **Richard Steele**, **Perkins**, and **Daniel
  Rogers** lean the same direction. **Palmer** takes the strongest version among the Americans: the
  Fall changes only the *mode of enforcement* ("positive statute"), not the content of headship at
  all.
- **Fall-grounded, explicitly**: **Cleaver & Dod** — "the first subjection of the woman began at
  sin," full stop.
- **Both-together, deliberately**: **Gouge** visibly wrestles with holding both grounds at once —
  Gen. 3:16 as punishment *and* "a law too, for trial of her obedience," plus a separate
  creation-order argument.
- **The most totalizing version — punishment, not mere intensification**: **Dabney** goes further
  than anyone else here: the wife's subordination is itself, in part, **punishment for Eve's sin,
  imputed to all women ever since** — not a pre-existing order intensified, but a penal consequence
  extended by imputation. Track this as Dabney's own distinctive, outlier move, not "the Reformed
  view." **Palmer and Dabney are the same region, the same era, and genuinely disagree** — worth
  naming as the sharpest same-strand tension this project has found.

**Answered, 2026-09-06, now that the chapter's content is confirmed**: Adams grounds everything in
this chapter in pre-Fall creation — marriage's goodness explicitly "pre-fall" (Basic 2), the
helper role in Gen. 2:18, one-flesh union and marriage's priority in Gen. 2:24, the reproductive
blessing in Gen. 1:28 — and **never engages Gen. 3:16 or the Fall anywhere in this chapter at
all**. That places him closest to the "creation-grounded" camp (Calvin, Perkins, Rogers, Palmer),
but thinner than any of them: he doesn't do the both/and work Calvin or Gouge do (holding
creation-grounding and fall-intensification together), because this chapter simply never raises
the Fall as a question. Worth noting why: chs. 1-2 already did the book's sin/hope work (see those
weeks' notes) — Adams may simply have located "the Fall's effects on marriage" there instead of
here, making this chapter's silence a structural choice, not an oversight. **Discussion question,
sharpened**: does grounding the helper role and marital priority entirely in creation, with no
stated fall-intensification, make Adams's implicit position closer to Musculus's "no rule-language
before the Fall" camp (if headship itself is fall-only) or to Calvin's "gentle subjection, then
servitude" camp (if it's creational but was better before sin entered)? The chapter's own content
doesn't settle which, since it never states an authority/rule claim explicitly enough to place —
it grounds the *helper role* and *marital priority* in creation, but doesn't argue for *headship*
as such the way Perkins or Gouge do. That absence is itself worth naming to the group.

## Where the sources disagree — mutuality: load-bearing theme, or backdrop?

Every figure this project has researched affirms mutual duty in some form, but weights it very
differently. **Baxter** and **Rogers** foreground mutual love/companionship most heavily — Baxter
opens his household chapters with symmetrical framing ("the first duty of husbands is to love
their wives [and wives their husbands]") before any differentiated-authority language at all.
**Gouge** places 45 sections of *common-mutual* duties textually and logically *before* any
sex-specific duty. **Calvin**'s reciprocal head/body "offices" language (1 Cor. 11) is his own
version of the same instinct. By contrast, **Dabney**'s argument is comparatively thin on
mutuality — polemical-occasional (answering 1870s suffrage debates), not constructive-companionate.
**Answered, 2026-09-06**: mixed, depending on which sub-topic. Adams's sexual-ethics material
(Basic 3) foregrounds mutuality as strongly as anyone in this project's bibliography — "neither
party has a right over their own body" is stated flatly, without a differentiated-authority frame
attached, matching Gouge's and Perkins's own due-benevolence casuistry (see "This week's outside
reading" below). But the "overlooked aspects" material (family-time priority, "head of the home")
is differentiated, not mutual — the responsibility is assigned to the husband specifically, not
shared. So this chapter doesn't cleanly sort into either camp the way a whole book might: it's
Gouge/Baxter-style mutual where the topic is the marriage bed, and differentiated where the topic
is guarding the marriage's priority against competing demands. Worth naming this mixture to the
group as its own finding, rather than forcing the chapter into one camp or the other.

### Basic 1 — marriage as covenant (Prov. 2:17, Mal. 2:14)

**Calvin, *Commentary on Malachi* 2:14 — CONFIRMED, direct quote, new to this project** (CCEL,
`calcom30`; 19th-c. CTS translation, not old-spelling). This is the single best available match for
Adams's own move: Calvin reads "the wife of thy covenant" as marking marriage's divine
authorization over against ordinary human contract — "when a marriage takes place... God presides
and requires a mutual pledge from both" — and explicitly cross-references Adams's *other* Basic-1
proof-text, Prov. 2:17: Solomon "calls marriage the covenant of God, for it is superior to all human
contracts." God himself is "the stipulator, who by his authority joins the man to the woman." Same
two verses, same underlying claim (marriage outranks contract because God, not the couple, is its
author), four centuries apart.

**Gouge, *Of Domesticall Duties*, Treatise II — CONFIRMED, direct quote.** Stated flatly, as one of
three grounds for solemnizing marriage publicly: "Mariage is Gods couenant, wherein he himselfe
hath a maine and principall stroke." Real "marriage is a covenant" language, though framed to
justify public solemnization rather than developed into Adams's fuller "blessings and sanctions"
doctrine. See `research/reading-notes/strand-2-reformed-orthodox-puritan/household-duty-writers-overview.md`
for the fuller quote and context.

**Perkins, *Christian Oeconomie* — checked, partial match only, honest negative noted.** Perkins
does call marriage a "couenant" repeatedly, but every instance located sits inside his chapter on
divorce for adultery/desertion ("adulterie... doth quite break off not only the vse, but the bond
and couenant of mariage," citing Matt. 19:9) — not a standalone doctrinal statement matching Adams's
"unilaterally imposed, blessings and sanctions" framing. **Don't cite Perkins for Basic 1
specifically** — Gouge and Calvin are the better sources for this exact point.

### Basic 2 — marriage is good, pre-Fall (Heb. 13:4, Eph. 5:22-33, Rev. 19:7-9/21:2, 1 Cor. 7:26, Gen. 2:18)

**Calvin, *Commentary on Hebrews* 13:4 — CONFIRMED, direct quote, new to this project** (CCEL,
`calcom44`). Calvin reads the verse as removing every exception, not conceding marriage as a lesser
good: it teaches marriage "is honourable," not merely permitted "as an indulgence, as Jerome
sophistically says." He names the verse's occasion explicitly: countering "a superstition... that
marriage is a profane thing, or at least far removed from Christian perfection" — an anti-monastic,
anti-celibacy-mandate polemic. Same anti-ascetic move as Adams's Basic 2, independently reached from
the same verse.

**Perkins, *Christian Oeconomie*, Chap. 10 — CONFIRMED, direct quote, and a genuine surprise
find.** Perkins independently cites this identical verse ("Mariage is honorable among all, and the
bed vndefiled") inside his due-benevolence chapter (see Basic 3 below), naming "Syricius, that
filthy Pope of Rome" and the Council of Trent as the targets of the same anti-ascetic argument
Calvin makes. Worth noting to the group: Adams, Calvin, and Perkins all reach for Heb. 13:4 to make
essentially the same point, across four centuries and three genres.

**Ephesians 5:22-33, Rev. 19:7-9/21:2, Gen. 2:18** — already well-covered in this project via
Calvin's Ephesians commentary, Zanchi, and Palmer's Ch. IV typological material (see
`week-01-christ-centered-home.md`); no new primary-text work needed for this chapter specifically.

### Basic 3 — why woman was created; mutual authority over the body (Gen. 2:18; 1 Cor. 7:3-5 territory)

**Zanchi, *Spiritual Marriage*, Ch. 1 — pairing re-confirmed, still the right match.** Already

needed.

**Calvin, *Commentary on 1 Corinthians* 7:3-5 — CONFIRMED, direct quote, new to this project**
(CCEL, `calcom39`). Calvin is more cautious than Adams's blunt formulation: he reads "due

### Overlooked aspects — the marital bond's priority over the parent-child bond (Gen. 2:24, "leave father and mother")

**Gouge, *Of Domesticall Duties*, Treatise I §80, "Of preferring husband or wife before parents" —
CONFIRMED, direct quote, and the single best primary-source match found anywhere in this project
for Adams's central claim in this section.** From the identical text Adams uses (Eph. 5:31/Gen.
2:24), Gouge states the priority as flatly as Adams does: "the bond of mariage is declared to be the
most inuiolable bond that can be... more firme and inuiolable" than the parent-child bond, because
"there was husband and wife before there was parent and child," and "no time, wherein man and wife
may part asunder till death part them." §81 applies this to a concrete besetting temptation — not
Adams's child-centered drift, but spouses who neglect each other to please their own parents/in-laws
("some wiues... priuily purloine from their husbands to bestow on their parents"). **Worth naming
the difference honestly**: same principle (marriage outranks every competing family bond), different
named temptation, four centuries apart. See the strand file for the fuller quotes.

priority-over-competing-bonds argument Gouge makes. Full text checked end to end; this is a real gap
in Perkins relative to Gouge, not an unfinished search.

### Garris, *Masculine Christianity* — a contemporary parallel to Adams's feminism polemic, checked and only partly matching

Adams's Basic 3 (above) makes a specific, dateable rhetorical move against the women's-liberation
movement of his own moment (1972): correctly inhabiting the Gen. 2:18 helper role *liberates* a
woman, while women's-lib, by subverting it, *enslaves* her instead — an inversion of the
movement's own claim to speak for women's freedom. Worth checking whether this project's
contemporary bibliography has anything that parallels it directly, rather than assuming it does.

**The check, run against the primary text where possible, not secondhand characterization:**
Zachary Garris does address second-wave feminism specifically and by name — this is real, and
closer engagement with a named historical wave of feminism than anything else currently documented
in this project's contemporary-interlocutor files (`research/reading-notes/strand-6-contemporary-interlocutors/`:
Mathis, Mize, Miller, Knight, Duncan, Phillips, and the Wiley/Wilson/DeYoung entries have no
comparably specific feminism-by-name material on record). In *Masculine Christianity* (2020), Garris
periodizes the women's movement into three waves — "the first wave of the 1830s to 1920; the
second wave of the 1960s to the 1990s; and the third wave of the 1990s to the present" (confirmed
directly against the book's own text via an online preview) — and characterizes the second wave
specifically: **"If first-wave feminism can be described as women's desire to be independent from
men, second-wave feminism can be described as women's desire to act like men"** (p. 17, quoted in
a secondary review — reforminganthropologycom.wordpress.com, 2022 — cross-checked against the
book's own periodization language in the preview; the quote itself has not been independently
verified against the physical page, so treat as WELL-ATTESTED rather than CONFIRMED until someone
checks a copy directly).

**But the claim-structure doesn't match Adams's, and that's worth stating plainly rather than
forcing the parallel.** Across the material actually located for this pass — *Masculine
Christianity* itself (preview and two independent reviews/summaries), the knowingscripture.com
Edgington review, "The Leaven of Egalitarianism" (American Reformer, 2023), and the *Honor Thy
Fathers* reviews (Themelios, TruthScript) — Garris's critique of feminism runs **definitional and
consequentialist**, not liberation/enslavement-inversion: he *names and dates* the waves and
characterizes what each one wants ("independent from men," "act like men"), and elsewhere argues
feminist assumptions about women's careers correlate with falling birth rates and women moving away
from motherhood ("The Leaven of Egalitarianism": criticizing Nancy Pearcey for treating women
"racing forward academically" as straightforwardly good). Nowhere located in this pass does Garris
make Adams's specific rhetorical move — inverting feminism's own liberation language to argue the
helper role is what actually frees a woman. **UNVERIFIED that Garris makes this move anywhere** —
absence of evidence in a real search pass, not proof it's absent from his full corpus (the book
itself remains not fully read cover-to-cover by this project, and his knowingscripture.com archive
is large).

**Verdict for discussion**: a real, sourced, dateable contemporary voice naming second-wave
feminism specifically exists in this project's bibliography (Garris), which is worth citing
alongside Adams as evidence this is a live, decades-spanning concern for Reformed writers — but
don't cite Garris as making *the same argument* Adams makes. Adams's liberation/enslavement
inversion, tied specifically to Gen. 2:18's helper role and dated to 1972's second-wave moment, is
still — on current evidence — a claim unique to Adams in this project's contemporary bibliography,
not corroborated by a matching move from Garris or anyone else currently documented.
