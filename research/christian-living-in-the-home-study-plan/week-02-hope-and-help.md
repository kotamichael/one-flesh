# Week 2 — "Hope and Help for Your Family"

## What's actually in the chapter (CONFIRMED 2026-08-19, via the project owner's direct read)

Built directly on chapter 1's diagnosis, and now confirmed in its specific shape: this chapter is
Adams's signature move — offering Christian **hope for change** by **rejecting the "medical"/
disease model** of counseling (the psychiatric/psychoanalytic framing that treats besetting family
sin as a fixed condition, a sickness, rather than a repentable pattern) and instead **calling the
problem what it actually is: sin** — which, unlike a "disease," can be named, confessed, repented
of, and forgiven. This is the same argument Adams makes at book length two years earlier in
*Competent to Counsel* (1970), his foundational and programmatic statement of nouthetic counseling,
here compressed and applied specifically to the family. See
`research/reading-notes/strand-6-contemporary-interlocutors/adams-marriage-counseling.md`'s ch. 2
section (updated 2026-08-19) for the fuller argument as Adams makes it across his corpus — direct
quotes on the sin/sickness distinction, its connection to hope, and 1 Cor 10:13's role in it,
sourced and confidence-marked. The chapter's own specific illustrations or worksheets (if any)
beyond this argument remain unlocated — that narrower question is still genuinely open, but the
chapter's core content and citation no longer are.

## Memory verse this week

**CONFIRMED (2026-08-19, via the project owner's direct read): 1 Corinthians 10:13 (KJV)** —
"There hath no temptation taken you but such as is common to man: but God is faithful, who will
not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able; but will with the temptation also make a way
to escape, that ye may be able to bear it." This replaces the earlier 1 John 1:9 placeholder, which
was offered only as a fitting supplement, never as a confirmed citation. The verse's own logic —
temptation as *common* (not exceptional to your family), *bounded* (not beyond what you can bear),
and *escapable* (God himself makes "a way to escape") — is precisely the hope Adams is arguing for
against the disease model: family sin is not a unique, unbearable, or inescapable condition.

## Who Adams is arguing against here

**CONFIRMED, sharpened 2026-08-19** (same occasion as chapter 1): the mid-20th-century dominance,
including inside pastoral practice itself, of the Freudian/psychoanalytic and Rogerian
(non-directive, client-centered) counseling models — pastors of Adams's own generation had been
trained to defer to psychiatric categories even inside the church's own counseling room. Adams was
blunt about the posture he rejected: "Think of the sorry picture the conservative Christian
minister makes as he sits behind his desk grunting non-judgmentally. The Christian pastor is called
to be a paraclete, not a parakeet." His own thinking was also catalyzed by (while explicitly not
identical with) O. Hobart Mowrer's prior secular critique of institutional psychiatry — see the
Adams research file for the fuller sourcing on Mowrer's influence and Adams's own qualification of
it.

## Cross-references — your physical books first

This chapter's general theme (hope for change within a struggling household) doesn't map onto a
specific marriage-roles text the way chapters 4, 6, 7, and 9 will — so the connections here are
looser, and worth naming as such rather than forcing a fit.

**Calvin's *Institutes* 4.12** (on church discipline, already used elsewhere in this project's
Strand 1 research) states Calvin's own rationale for why discipline exists at all: not mere
punishment but "that the sinner may be ashamed, and begin to repent" — restoration as the goal.
Not marriage-specific, but the same underlying theological instinct Adams's chapter is probably
running on (hope through repentance, not permanent diagnosis). Available in your Calvin set under
whichever volume carries the *Institutes*, not the commentaries.

**Gouge's *Of Domesticall Duties*** (per this project's Strand 2 research) makes a comparable move
in his dedicatory epistle: most marital misery comes from "an husband's abuse of his authority,"
not some fixed defect in either spouse — i.e., trouble is traced to addressable sin/behavior, not
an unchangeable condition. Worth a light mention if you want a 17th-century parallel to Adams's
"hope because it's addressable" claim.

**Adams's own *Christ and Your Problems*** (Baker, 1971, 29 pp. — published the year before this
book) is the closest same-author cross-reference available: an entire short booklet built around
1 Cor 10:13 as its organizing text, making the identical "your problems aren't unique, and God
provides a way out" argument at greater length. Worth tracking down if you want more of Adams in
his own words on this exact chapter's theme.

## This week's practice

Keep the homework simple and let your own reading of the chapter drive it: **write down, specifically, one area of
your family life you've quietly treated as "just how things are" rather than as addressable sin or
pattern** — the exact move chapter 1 and 2 together seem to be arguing against. Don't act on it yet;
just name it accurately. That naming is itself the nouthetic first step, per Adams's method (see
his "Method note" in this project's Adams research file) — identify the pattern against Scripture
before prescribing the fix.

**Heart-related**: Calvin's own language for discipline's goal — restoration, not punishment, "that
the sinner may be ashamed, and begin to repent" — is worth sitting with this week as the posture
behind the homework above. The point isn't self-flagellation; it's honest naming aimed at
repentance and hope.
