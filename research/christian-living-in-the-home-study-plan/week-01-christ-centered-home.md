# Week 1 — "A Christ-Centered Home"

## What's actually in the chapter (WELL-ATTESTED, via one named reviewer — confirm against your copy)

Adams opens by arguing that what makes a home *Christian* isn't circumstance but three specific
marks: Christians "more or less readily recognize and own up to their sins" (p. 11), "know how to
address their sins" (p. 12), and "experience progressive sanctification out of life-dominating
sins" (p. 12). This is diagnostic/doctrinal scaffolding for the whole book — no husband/wife
material yet, no confirmed Scripture citations recovered for this chapter specifically.

## Memory verses this week

**Eph. 5:31-32 (KJV)** — the anchor for the whole book's implicit claim, even though this exact
chapter doesn't cite it directly (Adams's own citations here weren't recovered):

> "For this cause shall a man leave his father and mother, and shall be joined unto his wife, and
> they two shall be one flesh. This is a great mystery: but I speak concerning Christ and the
> church."

## Who Adams is arguing against here

This chapter's "sinfulness of sin rather than the sickliness of man's fallen nature" framing is
not incidental — it's Adams's opening shot in a fight he'd already picked publicly in *Competent
to Counsel* (1970): against the Rogerian/psychoanalytic pastoral-counseling model that dominated
mainline and much evangelical counseling by 1972, which treated marital and family trouble as
psychological sickness to be managed rather than sin to be repented of. Worth naming this
explicitly in discussion — it explains why the book leads with a diagnostic claim about sin's
addressability rather than with marriage-roles doctrine directly. (This project hasn't verified
Adams names specific opponents in this chapter itself — the occasion is well-attested from his
wider corpus and public reputation, not confirmed as an in-text citation here.)

## Cross-references — your physical books first

**Palmer's *The Family*, Part I Ch. I ("General View of the Family").** The direct structural
parallel: both books open with a chapter establishing that the family's true character is
theological before it's practical. Palmer's Part II, Ch. IV ("The Symbolical Mystery of
Marriage") goes on to make the Christ-centered claim explicit and at length, working through Eph.
5:23-32 clause by clause — worth having open even though it's a later chapter in Palmer's own
book, since it's the fullest version in your library of the claim Adams's title gestures at.

**Zanchi's *Spiritual Marriage*.** The whole book's thesis, not one chapter: "For our entire
salvation consists in this spiritual and divine marriage" (dedicatory epistle). If you want the
deepest available primary-source statement that a home's meaning is Christ-centered in the fullest
sense — marriage as a *designed type* of Christ and the church, not just a useful illustration —
this is it. Read the dedicatory epistle and Ch. 1 ("The Creation of Eve and Her Marriage to
Adam").

**Calvin's Ephesians commentary**, on 5:31-32: "Christ has appointed the same relation to exist
between a husband and a wife, as between himself and his church" — the restrained, classic-Reformed
version of the same claim, useful as a contrast point to Zanchi's fuller typological development.

**Perkins' *Christian Oeconomie*** — less directly relevant here. Perkins opens from a
creation-order structural definition (higher/lower, rule/subjection), not a Christ-centered frame.
Worth noting as a genre difference: Perkins starts from creation order, Adams (per what's
recovered so far) starts from gospel/sin-and-sanctification. Both arrive at the same substantive
doctrine of headship — this project's method (see CLAUDE.md) treats that convergence as a finding,
not something either author needed to argue toward.

## This week's practice

**Primarily Adams's own homework style, adapted**: his diagnostic method (per *Competent to
Counsel*) is to name a specific sin pattern, not a vague disposition. This week's homework:
identify *one* concrete, recurring pattern in your own home — not "I need to be more patient" but
something as specific as Adams's own p. 11-12 marks would require: a moment this week where you
recognized a sin in yourself toward your spouse, addressed it directly (confessed it, named it out
loud to them), and can point to one small evidence of change by week's end.

**Heart-related, from Zanchi and Palmer**: both anchor the whole of marriage in Christ's own
"delights in His bride" (Zanchi, dedicatory epistle) and in marriage as a "holy shrine" (Palmer,
Ch. IV). Before this week's homework above, spend a few minutes simply considering your own
marriage under that description — not as a problem to fix, but as something designed to *picture*
Christ's own delight in his people. Let that shape the tone of the homework, not just its content.

## Synthesis

### How the outside reading informs what we have in Adams

Adams's chapter 1 is deliberately compressed: three diagnostic marks of a Christian home, all
"centered around the person of Christ (Col. 2:3)," stated as counseling-manual assertion rather
than argued at length. Palmer and Zanchi — a Southern Presbyterian systematic-devotional treatise
and a 16th-century Reformed typological exposition, writing a century apart and in entirely
different genres — independently supply the theological *argument* for exactly the claim Adams
states but doesn't unpack.

- **Directionality is the sharpest point of contact.** Palmer's Ch. IV is explicit that "St. Paul
  is not here explaining union with Christ by marriage... but he is illustrating marriage by union
  with Christ" — the now-confirmed "hieroglyph over the very portals of marriage, not to be
  deciphered until the key was furnished in the work of redemption" (see the corrected quote
  above). Zanchi's whole book runs the identical direction: "our entire salvation consists in this
  spiritual marriage," with Eve's creation read as *a type of the creation of the Church*, not the
  reverse. Adams's own move — a home is Christian not by circumstance but because its resources
  (admission of sin, precepts for addressing it, the means of grace) are "centered around the
  person of Christ" — is the identical directional claim in nouthetic-counseling register: the home
  doesn't explain Christ: Christ explains, and supplies, what the home needs. Three genres, three
  eras, one convergent claim — worth naming explicitly as a finding, per this project's own method
  (CLAUDE.md: convergence across strands is a result to notice, not an assumption to import).
- **Zanchi gives Adams's thinnest point real content.** Adams's mark 3 — "the means of grace exist
  only for Christian homes" — is asserted, not explained. Zanchi's cross/rib typology (Eve built
  from Adam's rib as the Church built from Christ's own pierced side, "water" and "blood" read as
  regeneration and salvation) supplies the actual Christological content behind "means of grace":
  it isn't an abstract resource Christians happen to have access to, it's Christ's own atoning work,
  concretely pictured in the first marriage. Adams presupposes this; Zanchi shows what's being
  presupposed.
- **Palmer's "family as school of subjection to law" section supplies the structural reason sin is
  addressable rather than a "sickness."** If the family is already, by design, a system of
  law-under-affection ("harshness of authority tempered by parental affection," "law... sweetened
  by affection" at every level), then trouble inside it is a malfunction *within a category the
  family already operates in* — moral government, not a mysterious third thing requiring a
  medicalized category. This doesn't appear in Adams's ch. 1 (which hasn't reached the sin/sickness
  argument yet — that's ch. 2, see `week-02-hope-and-help.md`), but Palmer's framing is worth
  holding in view now: it's the structural premise chapter 2's argument needs, arriving one chapter
  early in this project's own reading order.
- **Where the will actually changes.** Palmer's account of the wife's submission as "deeper than
  obedience... rise above the determination of the will and go to the will itself" locates real
  change at the level of the will/heart, not mere external conformity — the same place Adams's
  mark 1 locates it ("admission of sin... releases the pressure, facilitates confession"). Different
  vocabulary, same diagnosis of where the actual work happens.
- **The Perkins contrast (already noted above) still stands and sharpens by comparison**: Perkins
  starts from creation-order structure, Adams/Palmer/Zanchi all start from the gospel/Christ. That
  three-way convergence makes the Perkins difference more visible as a real, substantive variation
  in emphasis (per CLAUDE.md's "historical diversity is a feature" rule) rather than noise to
  explain away.
