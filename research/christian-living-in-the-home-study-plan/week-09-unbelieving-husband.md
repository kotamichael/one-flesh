# Week 9 — "How to Live with an Unbelieving Husband"

## What's in the chapter

Not yet directly researched by this project beyond its title and its presumed anchor text (1 Pet.
3:1-6, inferred from the title — not yet confirmed against the chapter itself). This is a good
week to read closely and compare against the exegetical/historical material below, since it's one
of the few chapters where this project already has a deep primary-source bibliography ready to set
alongside Adams's counsel.

**A related but distinct source, already researched — not ch. 9 itself**: "What Is She to Do?"
(*Journal of Biblical Counseling* 10:2, 1990; hosted at nouthetic.org) addresses a harder case — a
wife in a difficult/abusive marriage, not simply an unbelieving one. CONFIRMED content: she should
not "fear their threat, nor be upset" (1 Pet. 3:14); continue "fulfilling the marriage vow, which
included the promise 'for better or for worse'"; "a soft answer turns away wrath" (Prov. 15:1); in
acute danger, temporarily removing herself and her children to a neighbor's house is not wrong and
is explicitly framed as *not* separation; if the husband is a Christian, appeal to church elders;
Adams states he finds "no biblical warrant" for divorce or permanent separation on grounds of
abuse — reported here as Adams's own position, not this project's settled resolution of the
abuse-counseling question. Worth reading this article alongside the chapter, since it's Adams's own
extension of the same 1 Pet. 3 logic into a harder pastoral case — but keep the two cases distinct
in discussion; see below.

## Memory verses this week

**1 Pet. 3:1-2 (KJV)** — the presumed anchor for the chapter's title, and the source of the
evangelistic-submission logic this whole week turns on:

> "Likewise, ye wives, be in subjection to your own husbands; that, if any obey not the word, they
> also may without the word be won by the conversation of the wives; while they behold your chaste
> conversation coupled with fear."

## Who Adams is arguing against here

Likely candidates: any counsel that treats an unbelieving or difficult husband as grounds for
separation/divorce outside the narrow biblical warrant Adams recognizes, and — in "What Is She to
Do?" specifically — any counsel that would leave a wife with no concrete, safe course of action in
a hard marriage. Not confirmed as named targets.

## Cross-references — your physical books first

**Calvin's 1 Peter commentary.** This is the single most directly relevant piece of your physical
library to this specific chapter. On 3:1-7: wife's submission is evangelistically purposed ("that
they might by their probity allure their husbands to the faith"); "weaker vessel" should evoke
protective honor, not contempt; explicit spiritual-equality grounding ("heirs together of the
grace of life" means shared graces "in common"); marital peace as precondition for effective
prayer. Read Calvin's exposition of 3:1-2 specifically alongside whatever Adams says about living
with an unbelieving husband — Calvin is working the identical text this chapter is built on.

**Palmer's *The Family*** doesn't engage 1 Pet. 3 at all (a confirmed gap in his book, per this
project's research) — worth noting as an honest absence rather than searching for a connection
that isn't there.

**Perkins' *Christian Oeconomie*** — "weaker vessel" phrasing wasn't confirmed one way or the
other in this project's existing research (a large-file truncation left it unresolved) — this is
one of the two open gaps your physical copy can close (the other is in week 4/6's material). Worth
checking specifically whether Perkins engages 1 Pet. 3 directly.

## Other strand material worth knowing about

- **Bullinger**, *Christen State of Matrimony* — direct old-spelling quote, explicitly tagged "i.
  Pet. iii.": "Let the wiues be in subieccion to their husbandes, that euen they which beleue not
  the worde, maye... be wonne by the good conuersacion" — a period-English version of the exact
  evangelistic-submission logic 1 Pet. 3:1 states, and this chapter almost certainly assumes.
- This project's pastoral-practical lane treats "marriages under strain — abuse, persistent sin,
  unbelief in one spouse" as its own distinct application context, separate from ordinary daily
  life and pre-marital formation, with an explicit standing rule that headship never licenses
  abuse and submission never requires enabling sin — worth having that frame in mind if this
  week's discussion moves into harder territory than the chapter title alone suggests.

## This week's practice

**Directly from "What Is She to Do?" — the closest thing to Adams's own assigned homework for this
territory**: "a soft answer turns away wrath" (Prov. 15:1) as a concrete, practicable step, not
just a proverb to admire. For anyone in a hard marriage in the room, or simply practicing the
principle generally: identify one recurring point of friction and prepare, in advance, a specific
"soft answer" to offer instead of the reflexive response.

**Heart-related, from Calvin**: the wife's submission here is not technique but "probity" and
"chaste conversation" — character, not lecturing, is what Calvin says actually wins an unbelieving
husband. This week's heart-practice: resist the urge to argue someone into faith or change: let
consistent character do the work Calvin says Scripture assigns to it.

## Things to look for / discussion questions

- How does Adams's practical counsel in this chapter relate to Calvin's exegetical point that the
  wife's submission here is *evangelistically* purposed (aimed at winning the husband), not merely
  a general submission principle repeated in a harder context?
- If "What Is She to Do?" comes up in discussion, be ready to distinguish clearly between the
  unbelieving-husband case (this chapter's subject) and the abusive-husband case (that article's
  subject) — related but not identical questions, and this project's own pastoral-practical lane
  treats them as distinct application contexts for good reason.
- A good closing question for the series as a whole: across all nine content chapters, did the
  book give the wife's role — including her own characteristic warnings and temptations, not just
  her positive competence — the same depth of treatment the husband's role received? This is the
  question this whole study plan has been tracking from week 1.
