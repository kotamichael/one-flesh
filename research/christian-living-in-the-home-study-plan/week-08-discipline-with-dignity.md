# Week 8 — "Discipline with Dignity" (~pp. 108-125)

## What's actually in the chapter (CONFIRMED: parent-child, not husband-wife)

Every source located ties this chapter's content specifically to parenting, not marital
authority — worth saying plainly rather than forcing a marriage-roles connection this chapter
doesn't actually make.

> "Flaunting authority is always wrong... The brandishing of authority for its own sake shows that
> the parent fails to recognize that the authority is given for the benefit of the child... If
> 'His commandments are not grievous' (1 John 5:3), why should ours be?" — p. 114

> "The rod, carefully administered with love, meaning, and purpose... is the most merciful form of
> punishment." — p. 119

> "Discipline with dignity, then, involves not only structure... He was made in the image of God,
> and he must be reached in his heart with God's Word... [Parents] must continue to show them
> what He wants... not just with the rod, but also by the cross." — p. 122

Scripture engaged: 1 John 5:3, Deut. 11:1, Eden's one-rule-one-penalty pattern (Gen. 2:16-17),
an Eph. 6:4 "nurture and admonition" allusion.

## Memory verse this week

**Eph. 6:4 (KJV)** — CONFIRMED as alluded to directly in this chapter's own material:

> "And, ye fathers, provoke not your children to wrath: but bring them up in the nurture and
> admonition of the Lord."

## Who Adams is arguing against here

Two candidates, both plausible given the era: a permissive/therapeutic parenting model that avoids
correction altogether, and a harsh/authoritarian model that "flaunts" authority for its own sake —
the chapter's explicit "flaunting authority is always wrong" line suggests Adams is deliberately
positioning himself against the second as much as the first. Worth naming both rather than
assuming this is only a corrective to permissiveness.

## Cross-references — your physical books

None of your four physical books treat parent-child discipline as their subject — this project's
own bibliography is marriage-roles-focused, not parenting-focused, so this week's cross-reference
material is necessarily indirect. Say so plainly to the group rather than manufacturing a

**WLC 123-133** (the fifth commandment's material on "superiors, inferiors, and equals," already
the confessional anchor for this whole project's husband-wife work) — this project has now done
the dedicated research pass this week's draft previously flagged as outstanding, checking the
primary text directly (thewestminsterstandard.org's proof-texted edition, wording cross-checked
against the OPC's answer-only text). **CONFIRMED, not merely a plausible parallel:**

- Q124 defines "father and mother" as "not only natural parents, but all superiors in age and
  gifts; and especially such as, by God's ordinance, are over us in place of authority, whether in
  family, church, or commonwealth." Parents and husbands are both "superiors... in family" under
  this one definition — the Standards never give parent-child and husband-wife separate Q&A
  treatment. Both relations run through the same generic Qs 126-130, distinguished only by which
  Scripture proofs the Assembly annexed to each answer. That is exactly the same mechanism this
  project's confessional-reviewer lane already relies on to establish the husband-wife mapping
  (see `.claude/agents/confessional-reviewer.md`), so it applies here on the same footing.
- Q129 ("what is required of superiors towards their inferiors") draws its proofs from both
  relations at once: Col. 3:19 and 1 Pet. 3:7 (husband to wife) sit alongside Deut. 6:6-7, Eph.
  6:4, Prov. 29:15, Gen. 49:28, and Job 1:5 (parent to child) as proofs of the very same answer.
  "Parallel duties" is accurate and confirmed.
- Q130 ("what are the sins of superiors") is proved almost entirely from parent-child and
  civil-magistrate texts — Eph. 6:4, Col. 3:21, 1 Sam. 2:29-31 (Eli's indulgence of his sons),
  1 Kings 1:6 (David, of Adonijah, "his father had not displeased him at any time"), 1 Sam. 3:13
  (Eli again) — and cites **no** husband-wife-specific text at all. So "parallel warnings against
  abuse" actually understates the case in one direction: the Assembly's own proof-texts make
  Q130's caution against a superior's misrule *more* directly and abundantly evidenced for parents
  than for husbands specifically, even though the answer-text itself covers both relations under
  one "superior" heading. Worth saying that nuance to the group rather than flattening it to a
  simple "parallel."

## This week's practice

**Directly from Adams's own material**: examine one specific disciplinary interaction from the
past week or two — was authority exercised, per Adams's own test, "for the benefit of the child,"
or did it slide toward "flaunting authority... for its own sake"? Name which, honestly, before
moving on to anything else.

**Heart-related**: "not just with the rod, but also by the cross" — this week's practice for
whoever disciplines in your home: pair one act of correction with an explicit, spoken word about
Christ's own work, not just the rule that was broken. Adams's own chapter seems to insist
correction and gospel proclamation belong together, not sequentially.

## Things to look for / discussion questions

- "Flaunting authority is always wrong" — how does this compare to what this project's other
  research has found about *husbandly* authority specifically (Gouge's "abuse of authority" as the
  main source of marital misery, Perkins's "no power nor liberty... to be cruel")? The same
  underlying theological instinct — authority exists for the ruled's benefit — applied to a
  different relationship.
- Since this chapter isn't about marriage roles, use the week to discuss the broader household
  government question directly: how does the same "authority for the other's benefit, never
  flaunted, discipline aimed at repentance not mere punishment" logic apply across every
  relationship WLC 123-133 covers, not just husband-wife?
- "Not just with the rod, but also by the cross" — a good discussion anchor for how gospel-shaped
  authority differs from bare behaviorism, applicable well beyond parenting.
