# Week 6 — "A Word to the Wives" (~pp. 70-100)

The parallel chapter to ch. 7, and one of the two richest weeks in this plan for cross-reference
material — this project has spent real effort making sure the wife's side gets the same depth as
the husband's, across every strand.

## What's actually in the chapter (WELL-ATTESTED via independent reader accounts — direct chapter text still not obtained by this project)

Adams addresses wives personally and directly, second person: "...so that won't let you off the
hook; not for one moment," and "Yet by comparison to what Jesus Christ has required of their
husbands, Christian Wives have an easy lot" — his own comparative point being that the husband's
charge (Christlike, sacrificial headship) is the heavier one. Reviewers describe an extended
bread-baking/cookbook illustration in this chapter; one reviewer (Laura McCarter) judged the
chapter "biblically faithful." **The famous "buck stops... with the husband" line (p. 83) most
likely falls inside this chapter**, meaning it was probably delivered as part of Adams's case *to
the wife* for why the final say rests with her husband, not as a husband-directed exhortation —
worth confirming directly against your copy, since this project has flagged it `[VERIFY]`.

**A separately-recovered quote, upgraded from "probable hallucination" to WELL-ATTESTED this
week** (also cited to p. 83, per gracequotes.org): "The idea that women should not hold jobs is
false... [on Prov. 31] the key to whether a job is fitting or not lies solely in whether the job
helps or hinders her family." If this checks out against your copy, it's a genuinely concrete,
positive statement of a wife's own vocational discretion — worth flagging to whoever maintains
this project's Adams research note either way.

## Who Adams is arguing against here

Two candidates, in tension with each other, worth naming both: (1) a reduction of the wife's role
to bare submission with no positive content of her own — which the chapter's actual structure (a
full chapter, mirroring the husband's) argues against by its very existence; (2) possibly early
1970s second-wave feminist critiques of domesticity specifically, if the Prov. 31 "job" material
above is engaging that debate directly — plausible given the book's date, not confirmed.

## Cross-references — your physical books first

**Perkins' *Christian Oeconomie*.** Extended direct use of Prov. 31 as the wife's *proper*
domestic industry and competence, not decorative praise: "the heart of her husband trusteth
her... she seeketh wool and flax, and laboureth cheerfully." The closest primary-source parallel to
whatever Prov. 31 material Adams uses — read them side by side.

**Palmer's *The Family*, Ch. II-III.** The richest wife's-side material this project has found
anywhere, and worth having open this week specifically: "Within her sphere the wife wields an
**original authority**, which she acquires through her submission... She resigns independence, but
secures control." Palmer's "joint rulers" language ("the joint rulers over the domestic state") and
his "professional competence" analogy ("It is her function to preside over [the home], as it is
that of the judge to sit upon the bench") are the strongest available primary-source statements of
a wife's own positive governance — a good stress-test for how far Adams's own "easy lot" comparison
(above) actually goes, since Palmer would clearly reject "easy" as the right word for what he
calls "original authority."

**Zanchi's *Spiritual Marriage*, Ch. 5 ("The Duties of the Husband and of the Wife," pp. 87-104).**
This is the single most important unread primary source in this entire project — nobody has yet
gotten past the publisher's sample excerpt to reach it. This week is the natural occasion to read
it, since it's Zanchi's own direct statement of the wife's duties, parallel to whatever Adams says
here.

**Calvin's 1 Peter commentary**, on 3:1-7: wife's submission is evangelistically purposed ("that
they might by their probity allure their husbands to the faith"); "weaker vessel" should evoke
protective honor, not contempt; "heirs together of the grace of life" grounds a shared spiritual
equality alongside the functional order.

## Where the sources disagree — how much weight does "helper" (Gen. 2:18) carry?

- **Vermigli** reports a *live patristic dispute* rather than picking a side outright: Chrysostom
  denied any pre-Fall subjection at all; an alternative view (which Vermigli seems to favor) finds
  some order already implicit in the "helper" relation itself, pre-Fall, intensified after.
- **Oecolampadius** asserts a "made for him" teleology even pre-Fall (from the naming-of-animals
  narrative), while still holding "original equality" — a distinct middle position from both
  Vermigli's live-dispute framing and Musculus's flat denial of any pre-Fall rule (see week 4).
- **Palmer** goes furthest in making "helper" a dignity claim, not a subordination claim: being
  "taken from his very substance" makes it *incoherent*, in his view, to call the woman "inferior
  in dignity of nature" — helper-status and equal-dignity are not in tension for him. This is the
  same Palmer material as the "original authority" quote above — his whole account of the wife's
  competence flows from reading "helper" as dignifying, not diminishing.

**Discussion question**: does whatever Adams's chapter says about the wife's role lean toward
Palmer's "helper as dignity" reading, or toward a thinner, more functional sense of "helper"?

## Other strand material worth knowing about (not in your physical library, but real)

- **Gouge's *Of Domesticall Duties*, Treatise III, §18-31** — his most casuistic material on
  exactly which household decisions a wife may make without her husband's consent, and which
  need it. The strongest available historical parallel to a modern "what does a wife's authority
  actually cover, day to day" question.
- **Cleaver & Dod's Abigail concession**: "some women are wiser and more discreet than their
  husbands, as Abigail... Yet still... [she rests] in acknowledging their husbands to be their
  heads" — real competence acknowledged, formally subordinated, not denied.
- **Bullinger**: Eve as "the help or arm of man," not "an impediment or a necessary evil."
- **Katharina Schütz Zell** — a first-person Reformation-era wife's own claim to competence and
  calling, in her own voice: "my pious husband only called me his curate, although I never stood
  on the pulpit — something I did not have to do in my line of duties."
