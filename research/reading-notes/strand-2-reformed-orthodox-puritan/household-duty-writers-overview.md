# Reformed-orthodox / Puritan household-duty writers — overview

**Strand:** strand-2-reformed-orthodox-puritan

**Researched on:** 2026-08-06/07 (background research agent — EEBO-TCP/Bodleian OTA, Internet Archive, secondary scholarship where primary access was blocked)

**Note:** this pass explicitly asked for the wife's-side content with the same weight as the husband's side, per the project's standing discipline. The results below are notably better-balanced than the strand-6 contemporary material — several of these figures (Gouge, Richard Steele, Perkins) have real, concrete, positive content on a wife's own competence and authority, not just "she submits." ## 1. Thomas Cartwright — Proverbs 31 commentary now located and read

No dedicated household-duties treatise exists (confirmed via Chilton Powell's standard scholarly survey of the genre, which mentions Cartwright only re: church-government controversy) — that finding stands. But the promising lead flagged in the first pass has now been closed: *Commentarii succincti et dilucidi in Proverbia Salomonis* — accessed via a 1632 Amsterdam edition (the referenced 1617 first edition itself wasn't digitized; treat quotes as "the commentary, per the earliest accessible edition" pending confirmation the text is unchanged from 1617).

CONFIRMED (my own working translation from 17th-c. scholarly Latin — flag for independent verification by a Latin reader before quoting as settled): Cartwright's rubric for the whole chapter frames Prov 31:10-31 as depicting "the virtues of a **vigorous/capable wife**" (*uxoris strenuae*), not praise-by-contrast with vice. On v.11 ("the heart of her husband doth safely trust in her"), he makes it explicitly **mutual**, not one-sided: it is "the husband's virtue not to labor under jealous suspicion of his wife," paired immediately with her corresponding duty of manifest chastity. On vv.28-31, he draws an explicit **husband's duty** out of the wife's praise: to "deal generously with her in food and clothing... and see that children and servants pursue her with particular honor and reverence" — i.e., Prov 31 grounds a reciprocal husbandly obligation, not just admiration of feminine domesticity in isolation.

**Genuinely positive wife-content, and genuinely still UNVERIFIED on headship-grounds**: Prov 31 doesn't reach back to Gen 1-3, so this text says nothing about Cartwright's creation-vs-fall position — that remains unaddressed by any source found. Cartwright stays a contextual/ecclesiological figure for the headship question specifically, but is no longer evidentially thin on the wife's-competence question.

## 3. William Perkins — *Christian Oeconomie* (1609, posthumous)

CONFIRMED, direct quotes. Definitional move: marriage is a relation where one is "higher, and beareth rule," the other "lower, and yieldeth subjection" — stated as definitional before any fall-argument (Chap. 3, "Of maried folkes"). Grounds this in creation/sex-distinction via 1 Cor 11:7 ("the male is man of a superior sex, fit for procreation. The female is woman of an inferior sex, fit to conceive and bear children" — Chap. 5, among the "essential signs" of a valid contract, paired there with 1 Tim 2:12), not primarily the Fall. Restrains headship explicitly: "though the husband be the wife's head, yet it seemeth he hath no power nor liberty granted him" to be cruel. **Positive wife-content**: extended direct use of Prov 31 as the wife's *proper* domestic industry and competence, not decorative praise — "the heart of her husband trusteth her... she seeketh wool and flax, and laboureth cheerfully." Frames the household as "the school wherein the first principles... of government and subjection are learned" (the classic Puritan "little commonwealth" move). **"Weaker vessel" now checked against the full 1609 text directly (2026-09-06, EEBO/Internet Archive OCR, full-text grepped end to end through the book's own "FINIS") — zero occurrences.** This upgrades the earlier "not confirmed... large-file truncation" caution to a genuine, checked negative finding: Perkins simply does not use the phrase anywhere in *Christian Oeconomie*, not merely an artifact of a partial read. **2026-08-14: project owner has purchased a physical copy of *Christian Oeconomie* — pending his direct read to close this further, not an open agent task.**

**New primary content, 2026-09-06 — Chap. 10, "Of the Communion of married folkes, and of due beneuolence," recovered while cross-referencing Jay Adams's *Christian Living in the Home* ch. 4** (`research/christian-living-in-the-home-study-plan/week-04-bible-basics.md`). CONFIRMED, direct quotes from the 1609 EEBO/Internet Archive text. Perkins defines conjugal "communion" as mutual, obligatory, and reciprocal from the start: "that dutie, whereby they do mutually and willingly communicate, both their persons, & goods each to other, for their mutuall necessitie and comfort" (citing Eph. 5:28). The due-benevolence duty itself is grounded directly in 1 Cor. 7:3, quoted verbatim, and explicitly ruled "not of courtesie, but of due debt." Perkins **independently cites Heb. 13:4** ("Mariage is honorable among all, and the bed vndefiled") inside this very chapter, as part of an anti-Rome polemic — he names "Syricius, that filthy Pope of Rome" for teaching that marriage was "the vncleannesse of the flesh," and the Council of Trent for the same error, framing his own due-benevolence doctrine as a corrective to a real, named target. This is the same verse Adams cites for Basic 2 ("marriage is good"), independently landing in the identical due-benevolence context Adams's own chapter covers under Basic 3 — a direct four-century convergence on both the text and its polemical use. Perkins's casuistry: the act must be done "in moderation" (excess "is no better than plaine adulterie before God") and with "holy abstinence" on two occasions — during a wife's menstrual "flowers" (Lev. 18:19) and "in the time of a solemne fast," citing 1 Cor. 7:5 directly for the fasting exception.

**Covenant language, checked directly, 2026-09-06**: Perkins does call marriage a "couenant" repeatedly, but in every located instance the term appears inside his chapter on divorce for adultery/desertion, not as a standalone "marriage is a covenant" doctrinal statement the way Gouge states it (see below) or the way Adams's Basic 1 states it: adultery "doth quite break off not only the vse, but the bond and couenant of mariage" (citing Matt. 19:9). **Honest negative finding**: no passage matching Adams's specific "unilaterally imposed, with blessings and sanctions" framing, and no passage found linking marriage's covenant character to the priority of the marital bond over the parent-child bond — Perkins's own comment on Gen. 2:24/Eph. 5:31 ("leave father and mother... cleave unto his wife") is used only to argue monogamy ("two, and not three or foure, shall be one flesh") and the couple's own household residence, not the priority-over-children point Adams's chapter makes. Full-text checked end to end; this is a genuine gap in Perkins relative to Gouge on this specific point, not an unfinished search.

## 4. William Gouge — *Of Domesticall Duties* (1622) — the anchor text of this strand

CONFIRMED, extensive direct quotes (modern critical edition: *Building a Godly Home*, ed. Beeke & Brown, RHB 2013-14).

**Structure matters**: Treatise I is a 133-section exposition of Eph 5:22-6:9; Treatise II covers 45 sections of *common-mutual* duties (love, cohabitation, mutual prayer, care for each other's salvation) *before* any sex-specific duty — mutuality is textually and logically prior in Gouge's own ordering.

**Headship, non-degrading**: "Though the man be as the head, yet is the woman as the heart." The husband "ought to make her a joint governor of the family with himself, and refer the ordering of many things to her discretion" — direct textual evidence against a flattened "husband decides everything" reading. "Love is as sugar to sweeten the duties of authority... Respect is as salt to season... subjection" — every authority-duty is paired with a love-duty. Most marital misery, per Gouge's dedicatory epistle, comes from "an husband's abuse of his authority," not wives' insubordination.

**Positive wife-content — genuinely rich, this is Gouge's real distinctive**: Treatise III has an enumerated sub-section, §18 "Of the cases wherein a wife hath power to order things of the house without her husband's consent," and §21 on what she "may dispose without her husband's consent." §20 itemizes which expenditures need consent (children, servants, cattle, guests, journeys) rather than issuing a blanket rule. §31 works through a real casuistic question — "Where then is the preferment of the wife above servants and children, if she have not a property [in the goods]?" — distinguishing the wife's *dignity/rank* from mere property right. The dedicatory epistle explicitly carves out her "proper goods" and "extraordinary cases" from any consent-requirement. **This is exactly the casuistic specificity the pastoral-practical lane should draw on** — Gouge works disputed edge cases the way a counselor handles real marriages, not the way a system-builder states doctrine.

"Weaker vessel" (1 Pet 3:7) confirmed via secondary citation, used to argue husbandly forbearance, invoking Abraham/Sarah as the model.

**New primary content, 2026-09-06 — marriage-as-covenant, due benevolence, and marital-bond priority, recovered while cross-referencing Jay Adams's *Christian Living in the Home* ch. 4** (`research/christian-living-in-the-home-study-plan/week-04-bible-basics.md`). CONFIRMED, direct quotes from the 1622 EEBO/Internet Archive text.

- **Marriage as covenant, stated directly**: in Treatise II's discussion of why marriage should be publicly solemnized, Gouge gives three grounds, the third of which is explicit: "Mariage is Gods couenant, wherein he himselfe hath a maine and principall stroke. For God is the chiefest agent in ioyning man and woman in mariage." The other two grounds in the same list — marriage as "a kind of publike action" affecting "family, Church, and common-wealth," and marriage as "the most sacred and inuiolable bond" — sit alongside the covenant point rather than replacing it. This is real, direct "marriage is a covenant" language, though framed to justify public solemnization rather than developed into Adams's fuller "blessings and sanctions, unilaterally imposed" doctrine specifically.
- **Due benevolence, Treatise II, §9, "Of remedies against Adulterie, and in particular of due beneuolence, and of defect or excesse therein"**: Gouge's casuistry is sharper and more explicit than Perkins's parallel chapter. Denial is sin against a debt: "To denie this dutie being iustly required, is to denie a due debt, and to giue Satan great aduantage" — and he cites the punishment of Onan (Gen. 38:9-10) as proof of how gravely this wrong is reckoned. Excess is possible on both sides ("husband or wife is insatiable... wiues are much oppressed by their bedfellowes vnsatiablenesse in this kinde" — a rare explicit acknowledgment that a wife can be sinned against by a husband's excess specifically, not only the reverse), and abstinence is required against Piety (during a solemn fast), Mercy (during sickness — "shall Gods Sacrifice giue place to mercy, and shall not mans or womans lust?"), and Modesty (during menstrual separation, Lev. 15:19). This is a fuller, more casuistically developed match for Adams's "neither party has a right over their own body" than anything else found in this strand.
- **Priority of the marital bond over the parent-child bond, Treatise I, §80, "Of preferring husband or wife before parents"**: Gouge's argument, from Eph. 5:31/Gen. 2:24 ("leave father and mother"), is the single best primary-source match found anywhere in this project for Adams's "overlooked aspects" claim (the marriage bond outranks the parent-child bond). Gouge states it as flatly as Adams does: "the bond of mariage is declared to be the most inuiolable bond that can be... the bond betwixt husband and wife is more firme and inuiolable" than the parent-child bond, "for there was husband and wife before there was parent and child: and there is a time when parents & children may depart one from another... but no time, wherein man and wife may part asunder till death part them." §81 applies this practically to a real, concrete temptation — not Adams's "child-centered drift" specifically, but a structurally identical warning aimed at *in-laws*: spouses who "neglect their husband or their wife" to please their own parents, including wives who "priuily purloine from their husbands to bestow on their parents" and spouses who "can neuer tarrie out of their parents houses." **Worth naming the difference honestly**: Gouge's application is toward parents/in-laws competing for a spouse's loyalty; Adams's is toward children competing for a couple's time — same underlying principle (marriage outranks every other family bond), different besetting temptation named, four centuries apart.

**Creation vs. Fall — Gouge's full resolution, now recovered (Treatise I, via Chapel Library's transcription):**

> "**Object.** That was a punishment inflicted on her for her transgression? **Answ.** And a law too, for trial of her obedience... Besides, we cannot but think that the woman was made before the fall, that the man might rule over her."

Gouge holds **both** grounds together rather than choosing one, and his resolution has three distinct moves: (1) Gen 3:16 is punishment *and* an ongoing normative law ("for trial of her obedience"), not merely a consequence with no continuing force; (2) a creation-order argument stated as inference from narrative sequence — "the woman was made before the fall, that the man might rule over her," which he backs further with Gen 2:18 and 1 Cor 11:7-8; (3) a separate, fall-specific aggravating argument: "good reason it is that she who first drew man into sin, should be now subject to him, lest by the like womanish weakness she fall again." The creation-order argument does the heavier structural lifting ("we cannot but think..."); the fall-argument sits alongside it as a supplementary reason, not a replacement. This puts Gouge closer to Richard Steele's explicit "both together" position than "visibly wrestling" suggested — he resolves the tension, just more tentatively than Steele's confident proof-text stacking. Gouge follows this with six enumerated proofs for husbandly headship (Rom 13:1's ordained power; "nature" itself; titles like Lord/Master/Head; the Eph 5:23 Christ-Church typology; Gen 2:18; 1 Cor 11:7's head-covering) — his fullest single statement of the creation-grounded case.

## 5. Robert Cleaver & John Dod — *A Godly Form of Household Government* (1598)

CONFIRMED, direct quotes. Notably the most explicitly **fall-grounded** of this group: cites Gen 3:16 directly as instituting subjection — "the first subjection of the woman began at sin." Also gestures to Gen 2:23-24 companionate unity, so it's both/and, but the Fall is the foregrounded proof-text, more starkly than Perkins or Gouge. Defines headship pastorally: "defender, teacher, and comforter," not merely governmental. **Positive wife-content, notably candid**: explicitly concedes real cases of superior female judgment — "some women are wiser and more discreet than their husbands, as Abigail... Yet still... [she rests] in acknowledging their husbands to be their heads" — subordinating real competence formally rather than denying it exists. A wife facing a difficult command "may for her excuse gently propound the same" — respectful objection is provided for, not suppressed.

## 6. Richard Baxter — *A Christian Directory* (1673)

CONFIRMED. Opens with symmetrical framing: "the first duty of husbands is to love their wives [and wives their husbands]" rather than leading with differentiated authority language. Leading emphasis in Chapters VII-IX ("Mutual Duties," "Special Duties of Husbands," "Special Duties of Wives") is *mutual spiritual edification* — that characterization from the first pass stands for those specific chapters.

**Follow-up pass, 2026-08-08 — the Eph 5/1 Pet 3 gap closed, in a different location than expected.** Baxter does cite both texts verbatim, not in the household-duties chapters but earlier in the same volume, in his argument that family worship is part of God's solemn worship: "'Wives must submit themselves to their husbands as unto the Lord; and be subject to them, as the church is to Christ'" (Eph 5:22,24); "Arg. VI. 1 Pet. 3:7. 'Likewise ye husbands dwell with them (your wives) according to knowledge;' and Eph. 5:25,26. 'Love your wives as Christ loved the church...'"; "Arg. XIII. From 1 Pet. 3:7... 'giving honour to the wife as to the **weaker vessel**, and as being heirs together of the grace of life, that your prayers be not hindered.'" "Weaker vessel" also appears inside Ch. VIII itself, on the *manner* of a husband's duties: "in conjugal love, and tenderness, as over one that is tender, and the weaker vessel." **Revise the first pass's framing**: Baxter isn't avoiding these texts in favor of pure mutuality-language — he treats them as load-bearing proof-texts, just distributed across the *Directory*'s family-worship argument rather than concentrated in the household-duties chapters specifically.

**Positive wife-content, Ch. IX** ("Special Duties of Wives"): Baxter explicitly builds in space for a wife's better judgment even within a subjection frame: "you must **assist them with your better understanding**, in a submissive, and not a ruling, masterly way... A servant that hath a foolish master may help him without becoming master." A distinct route to the same underlying move Cleaver/Dod make with Abigail — real competence acknowledged, formally subordinated in manner, not denied in substance.

## 7. Daniel Rogers — *Matrimoniall Honour* (1642)

CONFIRMED, direct quotes — a useful addition beyond the original research brief. Grounds headship distinctively in the *imago Dei* itself: "the man doth especially resemble the image of God, and in that respect is the wife's head" — arguably the most purely creation-theology-grounded headship argument found in this whole strand (distinct from Perkins's 1 Cor 11:7 "superior sex" language and Cleaver/Dod's Fall emphasis). Notable companionate-marriage argument: shared religion is "fittest to level and equal" worldly "disproportions... in years, education, disposition, breed, estate" between spouses — an explicit leveling move alongside, not replacing, formal headship. The genre's most sustained positive theology of mutual conjugal *love* as an end in itself.

## 8. Richard Steele (1629-1692) — "The Duties of Husbands and Wives" (Eph 5:33 sermon)

CONFIRMED, direct quotes — and a real citation-accuracy catch: apuritansmind.com's hosting of this genuine Puritan sermon mislabels the author with **Sir Richard Steele the Whig essayist's dates (1672-1729)** rather than the actual divine's (1629-1692) — correct this before citing. (Also distinct from David Steele, 1803-1887, the American/Irish Covenanter — three different "Steele"s, don't conflate any of them.)

Content: holds creation-order and fall-confirmation together most explicitly and cleanly of any figure surveyed — "she was made after the man (1 Tim 2:13), from the man (1 Cor 11:8), and for the man (1 Cor 11:9)" stacked with "even after the fall the divine order stands: 'he shall rule over you' (Gen 3:16)" as confirmation, not origination. Vivid weaker-vessel language: "wives are like crystal glasses, easily broken if not tenderly handled." **Positive wife-content, strong**: "the house is her proper place; she is its beauty; there is her business and safety" (citing Prov 31:11 directly), plus an explicit *efficiency-based* argument for her household authority — husbands have "more important things to do than direct the household servants."

## Cross-cutting findings for the project

1. **Creation vs. Fall is a real, live fault line, not a settled consensus** — Perkins and Rogers lean creation-order; Cleaver/Dod lean explicitly on Gen 3:16; Richard Steele holds both together deliberately; Gouge visibly wrestles with the tension. Represent this range; don't flatten it.
2. **Mutuality-of-duty is universal across every figure but weighted differently** — Baxter and Rogers foreground mutual love/companionship most; Gouge and Cleaver/Dod foreground mutual duty-performance.
3. **Gouge is uniquely casuistic** — his itemized edge-cases are the strongest available historical material for the pastoral-practical lane's "how does this actually work day to day" question.
4. **Positive wife-content is real and recoverable in this strand** (Perkins, Gouge, Cleaver/Dod's Abigail concession, Richard Steele) — this strand, unlike the contemporary strand-6 material so far, does not have the imbalance problem CLAUDE.md flags. Use it as the model for what a properly balanced reading note looks like.
5. **Cartwright is the weak link evidentially** — no household-duties treatise located; his 1617 Proverbs commentary (unaccessed) is the most promising lead if the project wants to pursue him further.
