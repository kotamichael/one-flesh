## What he actually argues — husband's side (well-covered by this research pass)

Adams reduces headship to direct, actionable counseling directives rather than abstract theological proposition. From *Christian Living in the Home* (P&R, 1972, p.101), chapter "Loving Leadership": "You are to be the head of your home, including your wife, just as Christ is the head of the church... You are the head of your home. If there is little or no love in that home, it is your fault. God holds you responsible to introduce love."

On decisional authority (classic Adams formulation, widely quoted): "Somebody must have the final say... the buck stops not with the wife, but with the husband."

### Chapter 1, "A Christ-Centered Home"

**Updated 2026-08-17 — CONFIRMED via the project owner's own direct read of the physical book,
corroborating and extending the earlier secondary-source pass rather than replacing it.** Adams
opens (p. 10, per the direct read) from the observation that Christian homes are made up of
sinners like any other, then frames a complete view of salvation as justification (instantaneous),
sanctification (lifelong — delivering from the *power* of sin, as distinct from justification's
deliverance from sin's penalty), and glorification (delayed until death). Three components make a
home *Christian* (Christ-centered):

1. **Admission of sin** — releases pressure, facilitates confession, allows the sin to actually be
   dealt with. The earlier secondary-source pass (WELL-ATTESTED, via a named Goodreads review,
   "Zack," Aug 2020) independently supplies what's likely the verbatim wording behind this point:
   Christians "more or less readily recognize and own up to their sins" (p. 11 per that review;
   p. 10 per the direct read — a small page discrepancy not worth chasing further, per the project
   owner).
2. **Members of Christian homes have God's own precepts and examples for what to do about sin** —
   matching the earlier pass's "know how to address their sins" (p. 12).
3. **The means of grace exist only for Christian homes**, so there should be real, ongoing progress
   in dealing with the problems families encounter. The earlier pass's guess at this third point —
   "experience progressive sanctification out of life-dominating sins" (p. 12) — was in the right
   neighborhood (both concern real, expected change) but the direct read gives the sharper,
   correct emphasis: the *means of grace* argument, not a bare sanctification claim.

All three are explicitly centered on the person of Christ, **citing Col. 2:3** ("in whom are hid
all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge") — a real Scripture citation for this chapter that the

### Chapter 2, "Hope and Help for Your Family"

**Updated 2026-08-19 — CONFIRMED via the project owner's own direct read of the physical book**, following the same discipline as the ch. 1 update above (corroborating and sharpening the earlier secondary-source finding rather than replacing it). The chapter's central argument is Adams's signature move, made two years earlier at book length in *Competent to Counsel* (Baker, 1970) and here applied to the family specifically: **offering Christian hope for change by rejecting the "medical"/disease model of counseling** — the psychiatric/psychoanalytic framing that treats besetting family sin as a fixed condition, something closer to a sickness than a pattern — **and instead calling the problem what it actually is: sin**, which unlike a "disease" can be addressed, confessed, repented of, and forgiven. **The chapter's Scripture anchor is 1 Corinthians 10:13** (KJV): "There hath no temptation taken you but such as is common to man: but God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able; but will with the temptation also make a way to escape, that ye may be able to bear it." This closes both gaps the 2026-08-14 pass flagged as genuinely unlocated — the shape of the "help" beyond the bare hope-claim, and Adams's own textual anchor.

This confirms and sharpens rather than overturns the earlier WELL-ATTESTED throughline below: "because sin in families can be addressed, there is hope for the repentant" (pp. 15-19) was correct as far as it went; what's new is the specific logical shape of the argument (sin vs. sickness) and its Scripture citation.

**How this fits Adams's wider corpus** (WELL-ATTESTED to CONFIRMED via multiple independent secondary sources treating *Competent to Counsel* directly, corpus-wide corroboration rather than primary-text confirmation of this chapter's own pp. 15-19 — the owner's direct read supplies the chapter's specific shape; what follows is the fuller argument as Adams makes it elsewhere, which the chapter appears to be compressing):

- Adams traces his own turn to this argument to reading O. Hobart Mowrer's *The Crisis in Psychiatry and Religion* and to his own pastoral observation of institutionalized patients: **"From my protracted involvement with the inmates of the mental institutions at Kankakee and Galesburg...I was convinced that most of them were there, as I said, not because they were sick, but because they were sinful."** And: **"I came home deeply indebted to Mowrer for indirectly driving me to a conclusion that I as a Christian minister should have known all along, namely, that many of the 'mentally ill' are people who can be helped by the ministry of God's Word."** (Both quotes per RPM Ministries' 2023 history piece on Mowrer's influence on Adams, quoting *Competent to Counsel* directly — WELL-ATTESTED, not independently checked by this pass against the primary-text page.)
- Adams is explicit that he does not simply adopt Mowrer's secular framework wholesale: **"I want to say clearly, once and for all, that I am not a disciple of Mowrer or William Glasser...Their presuppositional stance must be rejected totally."** (same source) Mowrer supplied the diagnostic provocation (psychiatric "dogmas" are false, most "patients" are morally, not medically, troubled); Adams supplies the biblical framework Mowrer's own moral model lacked.
- Adams's core diagnostic question, converged on independently by multiple sources quoting *Competent to Counsel* directly (a Biblia.com preview page and a Goodreads-sourced synthesis both reproduce this line): **"where, in all of God's Word, is there so much as a trace of any third source of problems which might approximate the modern concept of 'mental illness'?"** — his claim is that Scripture recognizes only two categories of human trouble (the organic/bodily, and the sinful), not a third, quasi-medical "disease of the mind" category standing outside both.
- Against the "medical model," Adams argued (per the 1976 *JASA*/PSCF review by Oakland and a 2016 review at Defence of the Truth, both engaging *Competent to Counsel* directly) that locating a person's trouble in an external, quasi-medical cause — "parents, the church, society, grandmother" — removes personal responsibility, and therefore removes the very thing that makes change possible: a moral agent who can repent. The alternative "moral model" holds that "our behaviour determines our emotions" and that "problems may be solved…by confession of sin."
- Adams's own summary formula for the payoff (WELL-ATTESTED via Goodreads' quote compilation of *Competent to Counsel*): **"In nouthetic counseling, clients are taught to solve problems rather than adapt to them. There is a biblical solution to every problem."** And, treating static, unchanging living itself as a spiritual danger rather than a neutral fact of temperament: **"A Christian sins if he becomes a static, sedentary person who fears positive biblical change and frantically clings to the past... To resist sanctifying change is to resist and grieve the Holy Spirit."**

**Who Adams is arguing against, named specifically** (per this project's house rule on naming a source's actual occasion): this is not a vague or generic target. Adams's occasion is the mid-20th-century dominance, including inside pastoral practice itself, of the Freudian/psychoanalytic and Rogerian (non-directive, client-centered) counseling models — pastors of Adams's own generation had been trained to defer to psychiatric and psychological categories even within the church's own counseling room. He was blunt about the pastoral posture he rejected: **"Think of the sorry picture the conservative Christian minister makes as he sits behind his desk grunting non-judgmentally. The Christian pastor is called to be a paraclete, not a parakeet."** (RPM Ministries, quoting Adams — WELL-ATTESTED) His own model is also indebted to, while explicitly not identical with, Mowrer's prior secular critique of institutional psychiatry, which Adams read as confirming from outside the church what he believed Scripture already taught.

**How hope specifically follows from the sin/sickness distinction** — this is the connective logic the "Hope and Help" chapter title names, and it is what makes 1 Cor 10:13 the right anchor text: a "sickness" or "disease," on the medical model, is something that happens *to* a person — at best medically managed, never a moral category and therefore never a repentable one. Calling the same trouble "sin" relocates it into a category Scripture treats as addressable: nameable, confessable, forgivable, and changeable by the ordinary means of grace. Adams states the payoff directly, per Goodreads' compilation of *Competent to Counsel*: **"Knowing that there will be a way out, an end to the problem, is itself reassuring"** — language that echoes 1 Cor 10:13's own "way to escape" almost verbatim — and **"Personality can be changed. God, throughout history, has turned Jacobs into Israels, Simons into Peters and Sauls into Pauls."** Real change, on this account, is not wishful thinking or willpower religion; it is what Scripture's own sin-and-grace logic predicts, in a way "disease" language structurally cannot.

**1 Corinthians 10:13 specifically** (CONFIRMED as this chapter's own anchor per the owner's direct read; WELL-ATTESTED as a recurring, load-bearing text across Adams's wider corpus, not a one-off borrowed for this chapter alone):

- The verse's own clauses map with unusual precision onto Adams's larger argument against the disease model: temptation is "common to man" (not exceptional or unique to this family's sin pattern), it is bounded ("not...above that ye are able"), and God himself supplies "a way to escape." Each clause does work against the "fixed condition" logic of a disease model: not unique, not unbearable, not inescapable.
- This is not a one-off. Adams built an entire booklet, *Christ and Your Problems* (Baker, 1971 — published the year before *Christian Living in the Home*, 29 pages), around this same verse as its organizing text. Per its own back-cover description (reproduced consistently across Grace and Truth Books, Westminster Bookstore, and Christianbook.com listings — WELL-ATTESTED): the booklet's purpose is "to put problem cases into perspective, showing first that no one's problems are unique," then to offer "genuine hope based on God's promise in 1 Corinthians 10:13" and courage "to know that real solutions are found God's way," concluding with Paul and Silas's prison deliverance (Acts 16) as an illustration of God supplying "a way out" even in extremity. This is strong corroborating evidence — not primary-text confirmation of this chapter itself, but a documented, repeated pattern — that 1 Cor 10:13 was Adams's go-to anchor for exactly this "hope because addressable, not exceptional" argument, independent of *Christian Living in the Home* ch. 2.
- Adams uses the verse the identical way inside *Competent to Counsel* itself, in a passage on counselees who plead helplessness (WELL-ATTESTED via Goodreads' quote compilation, citing pp. 133-134): **"When clients think they are helpless, that some strange mysterious force is at work overpowering them, and they say that I Corinthians 10:13 does not apply to their case, the truth may be that they are not serious about wanting to do the Lord's will."** The diagnostic move is notable: for Adams, denying that 1 Cor 10:13 applies to one's own case ("my situation is different / unbearable / exceptional") is itself treated as a symptom of the very unwillingness-to-change he is diagnosing, not a legitimate exception the verse fails to cover.
- The wider nouthetic/biblical-counseling tradition Adams founded has continued to treat this verse as central. Contemporary biblical counselor Robert Jones (Association of Certified Biblical Counselors) calls it "the John 3:16 of biblical counseling" — used because, in a single verse, it gives a struggling counselee all four things Jones argues they most need to hear: their hardship is not surprising, it is not unique, God is faithful and present in it, and there is a real way through, not merely removal of the hardship. This describes the tradition Adams founded continuing to lean on the verse, not an additional claim about Adams's own words beyond what is cited above.
