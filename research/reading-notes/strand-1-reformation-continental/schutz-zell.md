# Katharina Schütz Zell (1498-1562)

**Full citation (primary corpus, not a single title):**
- *Entschuldigung Katharina Schützinn für M. Matthes Zellen, jren Eegemahel* [Apologia of Katharina Schütz for Master Matthew Zell, her wedded husband, who is a pastor and servant of the Word of God in Strasbourg, on account of the great lies invented about him] (Strasbourg, 1524). ## What the source actually argues

Katharina Schütz Zell is not a household-duty writer in the genre of Strand 2 (Gouge, Perkins) — she never wrote a treatise systematically laying out mutual marital duties text by text. Her writing on marriage is occasional and apologetic: defending a specific marriage (her own, to the Strasbourg reformer Matthias Zell) and, by extension, the legitimacy of clergy marriage as such, against Catholic celibacy law and against personal slander. Three distinguishable lines of argument recur across her corpus, so far as this pass could establish:

**(a) Marriage is a scriptural good, not a concession to weakness.** She grounds clergy marriage in "Holy Scripture, in both the Old and New Testaments" (per the GHDI/Brady rendering) against the canon-law fee system that let unmarried priests keep concubines for a bishop's tax while criminalizing honest clerical marriage. Her argument is polemical-legal as much as exegetical in the narrow sense — she is arguing that the church's own practice already tolerates what it condemns.

**(b) Her own marriage was a deliberate act of solidarity and public witness, not personal preference alone.** CONFIRMED: "I myself married a priest with the intention of encouraging and making a way for all Christians — as I hope has also happened" (Apologia, 1524, per secondary quotation of the McKee/Brady translation). She frames herself as the first woman in Strasbourg to do so, taking on personal risk (excommunication for the whole wedding party is WELL-ATTESTED) to "help win his and many other souls" (Brady/GHDI rendering).

**(c) Her own public, spiritual role — writing, teaching laypeople, visiting the sick and dying, corresponding pastorally — is a distinct calling that does not require or claim the pulpit.** This is the clearest instance in her corpus of positive material on a wife's own competence and sphere, not merely what she owes her husband. Late in life, defending herself against the younger pastor Ludwig Rabus's charge that she had overstepped, she draws the line precisely: CONFIRMED, "my pious husband only called me his curate, although I never stood on the pulpit — something I did not have to do in my line of duties" (*Letter to the Whole Citizenry of Strasbourg*, 1557, per secondary quotation). She accepts the structural distinction (she does not preach from the pulpit — consistent with 1 Tim 2:12, which WELL-ATTESTED secondary scholarship says she respected as a real limit) while asserting a wide zone of legitimate pastoral competence short of that line: visiting plague victims, burying the dead, writing consolation and defense on her own initiative and authority. Matthias Zell himself is reported (WELL-ATTESTED, via secondary summary, not yet independently confirmed against his own text) to have called her his "wedded companion" and "assistant" in ministry — language of partnership rather than mere subordination, though it is his description of her, not her own self-description, and should not be conflated with her voice.

> "What is weak before the world, that God has chosen so that He might put to shame what is strong."

## Exegetical grounds (if stated)

Not primarily a close-exegesis writer in the way Zanchi or Vermigli are. So far as located this pass, her scriptural argument in the Apologia runs by citing the general scriptural warrant for marriage (Old and New Testament) against canon law, rather than working through Gen 1–3, Eph 5, or 1 Pet 3 as a headship/submission locus specifically. **UNVERIFIED** whether she engages Eph 5:22-33, Col 3:18-19, or 1 Pet 3:1-7 directly anywhere in the corpus — none of the secondary sources consulted this pass quote her doing so. The one clear scriptural allusion found is 1 Cor 1:27 ("what is weak... to shame what is strong"), applied not to marriage roles but to her own standing as a woman speaking publicly — and 1 Tim 2:12, which WELL-ATTESTED secondary framing (not a located direct quote) says she treated as a real limit on preaching even while she claimed a wide pastoral competence short of the pulpit. This is a meaningfully different exegetical center of gravity from the household-duty genre (Strand 2), which works Eph 5/Col 3/1 Pet 3/Prov 31 directly and at length — worth naming as a real difference, not smoothing it into "she agreed with the household-duty writers." Her argument is closer to a defense of clergy marriage as such (an ecclesiastical-polity and general-scriptural-warrant question) than to a treatise on marriage roles.

## Bearing on the project

**Value:** she is a genuine primary-source female voice, writing in her own name, in the Reformation period itself, inside the Strasbourg reform circle this project already engages through Bucer/Capito/Zanchi. She gives the project something the household-duty genre cannot: a wife's own first-person claim to competence, calling, and public spiritual initiative, stated by the wife herself rather than prescribed to her by a male household-duty writer. The "curate... never stood on the pulpit... something I did not have to do in my line of duties" line is a precise, primary-source articulation of exactly the kind of "wife's own positive sphere short of the husband's/minister's distinct office" distinction this project needs for the wife's-side material CLAUDE.md requires — and it comes divided the same way WLC 123-133 and the household-duty writers divide it: a real limit (she does not preach), inside a wide zone of real competence and initiative that she claims as her own, not merely delegated.

**Friction/complication, stated honestly:** she does not give the project a worked exegesis of the headship/submission texts, and her primary preoccupation (defending clergy marriage as an institution against Catholic celibacy law) is adjacent to but not identical with this project's central question (what Scripture teaches about roles within a marriage). Drafts should not cite her as a Reformation exegete of Eph 5 or 1 Pet 3 — she isn't one, on what's been found so far. She should be cited for what she actually is: a primary-source instance of a Reformation wife defining and defending her own calling and competence, in a marriage she and contemporaries (per Matthias Zell's own reported words) described in terms of partnership ("wedded companion," "assistant") rather than bare subordination — which is itself a useful, honest data point for the project's account of what a wife's sphere looks like in practice, distinct from, and not a substitute for, the direct exegetical and household-duty material.

**Distinct from Strand 2 genre-wise:** where Gouge or Perkins prescribe a wife's duties from the outside (as male household-duty writers instructing wives), Zell asserts her own from the inside — a genuinely different kind of source that should be named as such in any synthesis, not merged into "the Reformed tradition also held wives could act with initiative."
