# Consistory and Marriage-Court Records — Geneva (Calvin/Beza) and Zurich (Ehegericht)

**Full citation:** see "Primary sources, in their own hand" below — this note covers a genre
(the governing marriage ordinances, the reformers' own theological writing on marital
discipline, and the archival court minutes those ordinances produced), not a single work.

**Strand:** strand-1-reformation-continental

**Researched on:** 2026-08-10

## Method note — sourcing priority for this pass

The governing principle for this note: **interpretive coloring comes only from the reformers
themselves** — Zwingli's and Calvin's own marriage ordinances, Calvin's own theological
rationale for consistorial discipline, Beza's own treatise on divorce — not from any modern
historian's framing of what the records mean, their significance, or the motives behind them.
Modern scholarship is used in this note for exactly two mechanical purposes and no others:
(1) **transcription/access** — the Geneva Consistory's own manuscript minutes are unpublished
16th-century secretary-hand French for all but their first three years (see below), so reading
any of that later material at all currently requires either French or a scholar's
transcription of specific folios; and (2) **citation-location** — pointing to exactly where in
Calvin's or Beza's own corpus (by *Corpus Reformatorum* volume and page, or archival volume and
folio) a given piece of primary text sits. Wherever a modern source is drawn on below, what is
reported is the bare fact transcribed (a date, a name, an outcome, a quoted line), never a
modern scholar's judgment about what that fact means, how typical it was, or what it says about
the tradition generally. Any interpretation offered in this note's own "Bearing on the
project" section below is this project's own, reasoning directly from the primary texts — not
imported from the secondary literature.

## Primary sources, in their own hand

### 1. The 1525 Zurich Marriage Ordinance — full English text obtained and read directly

**"Ordinance and Notice: How Matters Concerning Marriage Shall Be Conducted in the City of
Zurich,"** issued by the Burgomaster and Councils of Zurich, dated Zurich, Wednesday, 10 May
1525. English translation in *The Latin Works and the Correspondence of Huldreich Zwingli*
(also published as *Selected Works of Huldrich Zwingli*), available complete via the Online
Library of Liberty. **CONFIRMED — I obtained the full PDF and read the ordinance text
directly (pp. 99–103 of that edition).** This is the founding legal document of the Zurich
Ehegericht, in translation, not a modern historian's summary of it. It is the closest thing
this pass located to "the institution's own account of itself, in the reformers' own hand."

What it actually says, and does not say: the ordinance is entirely procedural and
jurisdictional. It states the problem it solves (Zurich residents previously had to litigate
marriage cases at the bishop's court in Constance "at considerable cost" and delay); it
establishes a six-judge bench (two pastors "taught in the Word of God," two from the Small
Council, two from the Great Council), sets court days (Monday and Thursday), and specifies
appeal only to the Zurich Council. The substantive articles that follow govern: parental
consent for those under nineteen, the invalidity of forced or clandestine marriage, public
witness and church registration of weddings, and the grounds that dissolve a marriage —
proven adultery (with the innocent party free to remarry), and, at the judges' discretion,
"greater reasons than adultery, as destroying life, endangering life, being mad or crazy,
offending by whorishness, or leaving one's spouse without permission, remaining abroad a long
time, having leprosy, or other such reasons, of which no rule can be made on account of their
dissimilarity." **It contains no statement of a husband's headship or a wife's obedience as
such** — it is marriage law (formation, nullity, dissolution), not household doctrine. That is
a fact about the genre of the document itself, read directly, not a gap in the search.

### 2. Calvin's 1546 Geneva Marriage Ordinance — his own statutory language

Calvin drafted this ordinance himself; it is collected in *Ioannis Calvini Opera* (CO)
10/1:41–44. The following is Calvin's own operative legal language, reproduced with a precise
CO pinpoint citation in John Witte Jr.'s two essays synthesizing Calvin's marriage writing
("John Calvin on Marriage and Family Life," and "The Marital Covenant in John Calvin's
Geneva," *Political Theology* 19 [2018]: 282–299 — both obtained and read in full this pass,
and used here purely to locate Calvin's own words, not for their own argument):

> "[T]he right to divorce belongs equally and mutually to both sides for both have a mutual
> and equal obligation to fidelity. **Though in other matters the husband is superior [to the
> wife], in matters of the marriage bed, the wife has an equal right.** For he is not the lord
> of his own body; and therefore, when, by committing adultery, he has dissolved the marriage,
> his wife is set at liberty." (Marriage Ordinance, CO 10/1:41–44)

CONFIRMED as accurately reproduced by Witte; the CO pinpoint itself not independently
verified against the original this pass. This is Calvin's own statement, in a governing legal
text he drafted, of both the fact of the husband's ordinary superiority *and* its stated
limit — the one place among the marriage-court material located in this whole pass where a
governing legal text names headship directly, rather than leaving it as background assumption.

### 3. Calvin, *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, Book 4, Chapter 12, "Of the Discipline
   of the Church"

CONFIRMED — read directly (CCEL text). This is Calvin's own theological rationale for the
kind of body the Consistory was, in his own words. Discipline, Calvin writes, is "as it were,
[the church's] sinews; for to it it is owing that the members of the body adhere together,
each in its own place" — serving as "a curb to restrain and tame those who war against the
doctrine of Christ," "a stimulus by which the indifferent are aroused," and "a kind of
fatherly rod." He sets out three degrees — private admonition, rebuke before witnesses, and
exclusion from the Table — and three ends: that God's name not be dishonored by the label
"Christian" attaching to shameful lives; that the good not be corrupted by "constant
communication with the wicked"; and that "the sinner may be ashamed, and begin to repent."
Worth stating plainly: this chapter addresses the church's discipline of its members
generally; it does not theorize domestic or marital discipline as a distinct category. Marital
cases were one recurring instance the Consistory handled in practice under this general
rationale, not a separate doctrine Calvin sets out here.

### 4. Theodore Beza, *Tractatio de repudiis et divortiis* (Geneva: Vignon, 1569; reprinted in
   *Tractationum Theologicarum*, 2:50–103)

Beza succeeded Calvin as moderator of the Geneva Company of Pastors and, on Witte's account,
"helped to complete and systematize" Calvin's marriage theology and law from the 1560s
onward — he is about as close, generationally and institutionally, to Calvin's own Consistory
practice as anyone who wrote a systematic treatise on it. **The Latin original is not
translated into English and was not read directly this pass** — content known only via
Witte's pinpoint-cited quotation of it. Witte quotes Beza's position, with page citation, on
desertion as a divorce ground equal in force to adultery:

> An innocent spouse deserted "in soul" (through the other's apostasy) or "in body" (through
> malicious abandonment) "has power either to forgive the fault and restore the marriage, or
> to condemn the fault and confirm its dissolution." Reconciliation should be sought "only so
> long as conscience allows." (Beza, *De repudiis et divortiis*, 88–99, as quoted in Witte,
> "Marital Covenant," 291)

This is desertion/divorce law again, not headship doctrine as such — consistent with the
pattern across every governing legal text located in this pass. **Open item, not yet checked:
whether Beza's treatise, in its fuller argument, states anything about a husband's authority
distinct from the divorce-grounds material quoted here.** The full Latin text exists on
Internet Archive and Google Play Books (see open questions) and is the clearest next target
for a Latin-reading follow-up pass — ahead of any further modern secondary reading.

## The archival case minutes themselves — an access note, then the bare facts

The ordinances above state the law; the day-to-day Consistory minutes record how individual
couples were actually handled under it. Getting to those minutes runs into a hard access fact,
not an interpretive one: **the Geneva Consistory's manuscript registers (Archives d'État de
Genève, *Registres du Consistoire*, R.Consist. 1–21+, 1541–1564+) are unpublished 16th-century
secretary-hand French, except for the first three years.** The only published critical
editions are an **English translation of Volume 1 only** (1542–1544; Kingdon, gen. ed.,
Eerdmans/Meeter Center, 2000) and a **French critical edition that has reached roughly
Tome XIII–XIV, into early 1559** (Droz, "Travaux d'Humanisme et Renaissance"). Beyond that —
which is where most of the 1550s–60s domestic material sits — there is currently no way to read
the manuscript except in French, in the archive itself, or through a scholar's own
transcription of specific folios.

For this pass, the only way to see any of that later material at all was through Jeffrey R.
Watt's *The Consistory and Social Discipline in Calvin's Geneva* (Rochester, NY: University of
Rochester Press, 2020) — an open-access monograph by one of the actual editors of the Droz
French edition. It is used here strictly as a transcription trail: what follows is the bare
factual content of specific dated entries only (a date, a name, what happened, what the
Consistory directed), with the archival locator preserved so a French-reading pass can go
straight to the manuscript itself. No part of Watt's own argument about these entries is
reported here.

- Two cases (1542; 1561) in which a husband was censured for violence severe enough to
  destroy a wife's eye: in both, alongside the censure, the Consistory's disposition also
  directed the wife to obey her husband and live at peace with him.
- **Jean and Jeanne Pradaire**, December 1561–January 1562 (AEG R.Consist. 18: 172r–v, 193;
  Jur. Pen. 183v): after testimony regarding repeated violence, Pradaire was excluded from the
  Supper and jailed three days by the Council; four weeks later, on his own complaint, the
  Consistory directed Jeanne to obey her husband and the couple to live together in peace.
- **Claude De Luc and Jeanne de Sales**, March 1560: both parties were admonished to "live
  well" together; De Luc's return to communion was made conditional on reform.
- **Humbert Revilliod and Huguine**, 1552 and 1556: after the Council briefly jailed Revilliod
  following a violent episode, admonitions toward reconciliation were addressed to both.
- **Claude Gindro**, August 1542: an early case (within the one published English volume) in
  which a husband admitted beating his wife.
- **Catherine Passirame**, February 1560 (AEG R.Consist. 17: 6r–v): granted a divorce in a
  single session on testimony of her own good conduct and her husband's seven- to eight-year
  desertion — an instance of the Marriage Ordinance's stated equal right to sue for divorce
  (§2 above) actually being exercised by a wife.
- **Jeanne Fassoret**, a widow who practiced as a healer, appeared before the Consistory in
  1547, 1550, 1551, and 1553 regarding her medical practice and gave testimony in her own
  right on remedies and diagnoses.
- Widows **Clauda Darbignier** (1564) and **Françoise Chevillion** (1556) conducted lending
  and land transactions in their own name and answered to the Consistory directly, not through
  a husband.

For Zurich specifically, no comparable case-level material was located in English at all in
this pass; the standard critical study of the Ehegericht's actual caseload, Walther Köhler,
*Zürcher Ehegericht und Genfer Konsistorium*, 2 vols. (Leipzig, 1932–1942), is German-only and
was not accessed. This is the single largest remaining gap in this note.

## Bearing on the project

Read together, the reformers' own governing texts give the pastoral-practical lane real,
load-bearing color, on their own terms: Zwingli's Ordinance shows what the first Reformed
civil marriage court was actually built to do (fast, cheap, local adjudication of
formation/nullity/dissolution, under joint pastoral-and-magisterial judgment); Calvin's
Marriage Ordinance shows him stating the husband's ordinary superiority as a working legal
premise while explicitly bounding it in one concrete domain (the marriage bed); the Institutes
shows Calvin's own account of why any of this discipline exists at all — restoration and
repentance, not mere punishment; and Beza's treatise shows the next generation extending the
innocent party's standing symmetrically to husband and wife alike in desertion cases. Across
all four governing texts, headship is assumed as a working premise, never argued for — the
ordinances argue procedure and grounds for dissolution, not the case for headship itself. That
tells the project something useful in its own right: the constructive case for headship
belongs to Calvin's exegesis and the household-duty literature proper (Strands 1–2), not to
the marriage-court genre, which simply presupposes it.

The archival entries (accessed only through the transcription layer, reported here as bare
fact) add concrete texture on two fronts the project cares about: how a wife's call to obey
and to seek reconciliation was pastorally pressed even in hard providences, and how the
ordinances' stated symmetry of standing (Calvin's "equal right," Beza's applying divorce
grounds to husband and wife alike) was actually exercised in practice — Catherine Passirame's
own successful divorce suit being the clearest instance located. Both belong in the project's
eventual pastoral material on perseverance and on a wife's own standing and agency.
